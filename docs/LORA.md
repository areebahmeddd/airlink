# The LoRa Trunk

Two Airlink nodes (Airhop's relay firmware) with SX1262 radios carry text between their Bluetooth meshes over LoRa, with no phone between them. This page covers what crosses, how, and the radio rules each region profile meets.

## What Crosses

At SF10 and 125 kHz the trunk moves about a kilobit a second under a duty limit, so it carries only text and the packets text depends on (`ah_trunk_policy.c`):

- **Crosses:** announces (one per sender per 5 minutes, 20 in India and 50 in Southeast Asia, with the node's own and one carried ahead of its sender's message at top priority), Noise handshakes, small encrypted DMs, courier envelopes, public, location channel and group messages, board posts, leave, ping and pong.
- **Never crosses:** voice, video, files and their fragments, sync traffic, OTA, prekey bundles.

Encrypted types are opaque, so a size ceiling and a per-sender token bucket (four packets, then one every 15 s) keep media inside encryption off the trunk without reading it. A brisk exchange of DMs, each with its delivered and read receipts, can run past that bucket and wait; at the trunk's rate it would wait anyway. A message whose sender's announce has not crossed in the last 45 s takes that announce with it, ahead of it, so the far side can verify it; a public message, location channel message or leave whose sender's announce the node does not hold (any made-up sender's, a phone's whose only announce came before the clock synced, or one learned only from a sync reply, until its live announce arrives) stays off the trunk, since the far side would drop it. That narrows a flood under made-up senders rather than closing it: the same message cut into fragments, and the other public types (encrypted channels, groups, board posts, pings), still cross within the duty budget.

Each LoRa neighbor is a link of its own in the mesh: dedup, fanout and priority are the Bluetooth ones, and multi-hop over LoRa is the ordinary TTL flood, one trunk hop at a time. A neighbor the mesh has not verified (by its own signed announce) gets only the node's announce, in one round with no probe, and everything sent to unverified neighbors together (that announce, answers to what they send, and beacons owed to nodes that do not know us) spends at most a quarter of the duty budget: any radio can make a fresh identity and beacon under it, and each then costs the node one announce frame (two where the announce interval is shorter than the 17 minutes a silent neighbor is kept), within that quarter, and never the airtime its verified neighbors use. A LoRa link binds only to the node whose key its frames are tagged under (below), and only to an announce that says it is a node, so no trunk source can take a phone's identity. One whose transfers fail twice is held down (below).

## The Link Layer

Frames are version 1 of the trunk frame, which only Airlink nodes speak (`ah_trunk_frame.h` lays out every byte). Each opens with the magic `41 4C` ("AL"), the version, the kind (data, acknowledgement, complete or beacon), a flag on a piece that asks for an answer, the power the sender transmitted this frame at, and the source node ID (its bitchat peer ID). A beacon adds the node's static key and a tag for each neighbor it knows; a data frame adds a 32-bit transfer number, the piece's index, the piece count, a tag for each destination, and one piece of a bitchat packet; an acknowledgement or a COMPLETE is 28 bytes: the transfer, the bitmap of the pieces held, the count and one tag. The radio's CRC covers the whole frame, so the frame carries none. A bitchat packet of up to 512 bytes goes in up to three equal pieces.

- **Authentication.** A node's bitchat peer ID is the hash of its static X25519 key, and its beacon carries that key, so the ID names the key. Two nodes that have heard each other's beacons hold the same pair key, from X25519 between their static keys, with no handshake and no airtime of its own. Every frame carries, for each node it is for, an 8-byte tag: HMAC-SHA256 under their pair key over every byte of the frame but its tags. A node acts on a frame only when one of its tags is its own, so only the holder of a source ID's secret can end a transfer, deliver a packet, claim a power or keep a link alive under that ID; a tag cannot move to another frame, since it covers the transfer number, the bitmap and the power. A beacon's key is the one thing taken untagged, since it is how a node first learns another's key, and says only that a node is there; the tag a beacon carries for each neighbor is what keeps that neighbor's link alive. A node that does not know us (a beacon with no tag for us, or a tagged frame from a source we hold no key for, as after either side reboots) is owed our beacon within 1 to 10 s, put off until 30 s after our last if that was sooner, so both hold the key again within seconds; so is a known neighbor nothing tagged for us has come from for an interval, whose beacon may have been too full to tag us. What the frames carry is bitchat packets the mesh verifies end to end; the tags protect what the trunk itself acts on.
- **One packet to several neighbors.** A packet the mesh queues for several trunk neighbors at once (a flood) goes as one transfer to all of them: its copies wait 20 ms to join, each piece carries a tag per destination, at most seven, and the destinations answer the round's asking piece in turn, in the order of their tags, an answer's airtime and 100 ms apart; no other piece is answered, since the sender is sending the next. A round sends every piece any destination still lacks; one that completes drops out of the tags, and one that stays silent is given up on alone. Nothing of the sender's own goes while its answers are due, and nothing of a receiver's while its turn to answer is still to come. Ten nodes in mutual range carry a public message in at most twenty transfers (up to eight neighbors each, seven to a transfer), not up to eighty.

- **ARQ.** Selective repeat: a round sends the missing pieces, the last asking for an answer; each destination's answer is the bitmap of the pieces it holds, or COMPLETE. The bitmap replaces what the receiver said before, so a receiver that lost the transfer (a reboot, an expired or evicted slot) is sent every piece it lacks again. An unanswered round is followed by a probe, one missing piece rotating through the missing ones, not by every piece again, after a random part of a short frame so two senders that collided once do not collide again. Twelve rounds at most; six unanswered in a row give up on a destination, and one does for a neighbor the mesh has not verified. A Monte Carlo of exactly this state machine at 30 % frame loss delivers about 99 % of three-piece packets, and the sender learns of about 97 % (the rest lose every COMPLETE). The host test runs it at 30 % loss.
- **Timing.** The feedback timer starts when the frame has left the radio, not when it was queued, so a wait for a clear channel never counts against the far side, and runs for every destination's turn. Acknowledgements go in their turn without listening first, the first at once. Transfer numbers start at a random value each boot, so a receiver still holding a transfer from before a reboot never takes a new one for it.
- **Priority.** Announces carried ahead of a message and the node's own first, then handshakes, DMs and mail, public messages, a phone's periodic announce, the rest. The mesh orders what it hands the trunk; the trunk holds at most four packets per neighbor and never displaces one it accepted.
- **Duty budget.** Every emission (data, retransmissions, acknowledgements, beacons) is charged against a rolling hour, in one-minute buckets, before it goes, its airtime rounded up to the millisecond. Data and probes may spend nine tenths of the budget, acknowledgements and beacons all of it: a node out of airtime still answers and still beacons, so its neighbors keep the link and keep reaching it, and only its own sending waits. Acknowledgements keep to a third of the budget between them, so pieces replayed to earn them never take the whole hour. A frame the budget holds back is offered again when a minute rolls off. A frame the budget cannot cover waits, except an acknowledgement not sent within 12 s, which is dropped: its sender has asked again by then. Of the eight acknowledgements that may wait, one for a verified neighbor displaces one for an unverified source.
- **Across resets.** The ledger is kept in RTC memory, which a restart, a panic, a watchdog and a brownout leave alone, so a node that resets in a loop never starts a boot with a fresh hour; the reset itself counts as no time. A power-on clears RTC memory, so the ledger is also written to flash (the settings partition) as the trunk starts and whenever what that copy lacks reaches a sixty-first of the budget. A power-on restores that copy as if no time had passed and charges the current minute with the most it can lack, a sixty-first and a frame, so a node whose power is cut however often keeps to the limit, and a fast loop silences itself. The copy is not rewritten as old minutes age out, so a power-on after a busy hour and a quiet one restores the busy hour and can hold the node, beacons included, for up to an hour: silence is the safe error. Only with no copy in flash is the hour before taken as spent at the limit, evenly: the node sends nothing until its first minute ends and then frees a sixty-first of the budget a minute, so its first beacon waits up to a minute and its data about seven.
- **Listen before talk.** A channel activity check precedes each data frame; a busy channel is checked again after a backoff of half a short frame to one full frame, and after four busy checks the frame goes anyway. No region rule here requires listening alongside the duty limit, and a channel that never reads clear must not silence the node. Neither a check nor a transmission starts while a frame whose header the radio has accepted is still arriving, since leaving receive would destroy it: data waits, listening, and an acknowledgement is offered again a moment later. A header the radio never finishes is forgotten after the longest frame's airtime, so only real frames back to back hold data for longer, and those are the channel in use: neighbors held to their duty shares and checking first leave gaps, and a source that leaves none is jamming, against which sending anyway would only destroy the frame arriving and collide at its receivers.
- **Beacons.** A beacon, 47 bytes and 8 more for each neighbor it is tagged for, introduces a node: 2 s plus up to 10 s after boot, so nodes powered up together do not collide, then 5 minutes plus up to 30 s after the neighbor this node tagged a frame for longest ago, and never sooner than that after the last beacon. A beacon tags at most seven neighbors, those tagged longest ago, so with a full table of eight the one left out goes in the next; ties among those the last beacon tagged break from a slot that moves on each beacon, so the one left out takes its turn and none waits more than two intervals. A frame tagged for a neighbor keeps it, so a trunk carrying traffic to every neighbor sends no beacons. The interval is fixed rather than configured, so no two nodes disagree on it. A neighbor silent for 17 minutes (three intervals with their jitter, and 30 s for a beacon held behind answers or a busy channel) is gone: two beacons in a row that do not tag it keep it (two lost, or with a full table one lost and one that left it out), a third drops it.
- **Hold-down.** Two failed transfers in a row drop a neighbor, and its beacons alone do not bring it back for 10 minutes, doubling each time it fails again, up to 2 hours. A data frame or an answer it tags for this node shows it hears us and ends the hold immediately, checked under the pair key the hold keeps; a successful transfer forgets the count. Without it, a node heard but unable to hear back (an uneven link) would return with every beacon and cost two failed transfers each time, most of a tight region's budget.
- **Adaptive power.** Where the region requires it (India), the node transmits at the power its addressed neighbor needs (the most any of them needs, for a piece to several), from the power that neighbor's last frame says it was sent at: that power, less how far above the demodulation floor and a 10 dB margin it was heard, never under 2 dBm or over the ceiling. A beacon takes the most any neighbor needs. The path loss is the same both ways; the powers at the two ends need not be, which is why the frame carries its own. The noise at the two ends need not be the same either: once a round to a neighbor goes unanswered, the node sends to it at the ceiling until a transfer gets through. A neighbor's power is taken only from a frame tagged for us, or from the beacon that introduced it, and is held between 2 dBm and the region's ceiling whatever it claims.
- **Radio watchdog.** A channel check or transmission that never finishes resets and reconfigures the SX1262 in place; one that will not recover is retried every 30 s. A radio that resets on its own (a brownout or a spike on its rail) raises no interrupt and keeps a sync word that reads as ours (0x1424 is the register's reset value), so before each channel check and transmission, and every 5 s while it listens, the node asks for its packet type and mode, and reconfigures one that is no longer set up or no longer receiving. The node never reboots for the radio.
- **What authentication leaves.** A radio in range can still jam the channel, or replay what it captured: any tagged frame keeps a gone node in the table until its transfers fail and it is held down, and a replayed piece delivers a bitchat packet the mesh drops as a duplicate, or the phones as stale, and earns an answer, which keeps to the third of the budget answers have. The tags do not cover their own order, so a replay may move a node's tag to a later turn; an answer still waiting keeps its turn, since no honest sender asks again before its turns have passed, so a replay never holds the node's own frames back for good, only a few seconds at a time, as jamming would. Replayed frames tagged for a node also set the power and signal it takes for that neighbor (India's adaptive power), costing at most a round at the ceiling after each. It can also make fresh identities, each a real node as far as the trunk can tell: they keep to the quarter of the budget for unverified neighbors, and only verified neighbors fill the table for good, which an attacker can hold only by transmitting from in range, at a cost that jamming would meet more cheaply.
- **After a reboot, and at a first meeting.** Either side's neighbors know it again within seconds (Authentication, above), but its mesh holds each LoRa link unbound until that neighbor's own announce arrives; until then the link carries only announces. The announce each node sends as a link opens usually arrives before its key and is dropped, so once a node's link binds (the far node's announce arrived tagged for it, so the far node holds its key) it sends its announce again, and the far side binds within seconds rather than an announce interval later.

## Airtime

AN1200.13's formula, CR 4/5, explicit header, CRC, 8-symbol preamble; the coding rate and preamble are fixed, since every node must use the same (`ah_lora_airtime.c`; `test_the_documented_airtime_table` checks this table):

| Frame     | SF10, 125 kHz (EU, India) | SF12, 500 kHz (US, Australia and New Zealand) | SF9, 125 kHz (Southeast Asia) | Example                     |
| --------- | ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| 28 bytes  | 0.41 s                    | 0.37 s                                        | 0.23 s                        | Acknowledgement             |
| 55 bytes  | 0.66 s                    | 0.58 s                                        | 0.35 s                        | Beacon, one neighbor        |
| 64 bytes  | 0.70 s                    | 0.62 s                                        | 0.39 s                        | Ping                        |
| 128 bytes | 1.23 s                    | 1.07 s                                        | 0.68 s                        | Short signed public message |
| 200 bytes | 1.85 s                    | 1.56 s                                        | 1.00 s                        | Signed announce             |
| 255 bytes | 2.30 s                    | 1.93 s                                        | 1.25 s                        | Full trunk frame            |

SF12 at 500 kHz has about the sensitivity of SF10 at 125 kHz; SF9 at 125 kHz gives up 2.5 dB of it for half the airtime.

## Regions

There is no default region. A node transmits on LoRa only once provisioned with `region us`, `eu`, `in`, `anz` or `sea` at its console; until then the trunk stays off, so no build is illegal anywhere by default. Each profile is one fixed channel, chosen to comply as configured.

| Region | Channel                        | Modulation    | Power ceiling                             | Duty               | Rule                                                                                                                                                                         |
| ------ | ------------------------------ | ------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `us`   | 919.0 MHz                      | SF12, 500 kHz | 28 dBm conducted, 34 dBm e.i.r.p.         | 25 % (fair share)  | 47 CFR 15.247(a)(2), (b)(3), (b)(4), (e)                                                                                                                                     |
| `eu`   | 869.525 MHz (869.40 to 869.65) | SF10, 125 kHz | 500 mW e.r.p. (29.15 dBm e.i.r.p.)        | 10 % over an hour  | ERC/REC 70-03 annex 1 band h1.7; EU 2025/105 band 54; EN 300 220-2 V3.3.1                                                                                                    |
| `in`   | 866.6 MHz                      | SF10, 125 kHz | 500 mW e.r.p., adaptive power control     | 2.5 % over an hour | GSR 853(E) of 2021, Table II (865 to 868 MHz; a non-access-point device)                                                                                                     |
| `anz`  | 919.0 MHz                      | SF12, 500 kHz | 1 W e.i.r.p., 25 mW e.i.r.p. in any 3 kHz | 25 % (fair share)  | Australia: LIPD Class Licence 2025 (F2025L01047), Schedule 1 Part 8 Table 8 item 6. New Zealand: GURL for Short Range Devices Notice 2022                                    |
| `sea`  | 922.0 MHz                      | SF9, 125 kHz  | 25 mW e.r.p. (16.13 dBm e.i.r.p.)         | 1 % over an hour   | Singapore IMDA TS SRD; Malaysia Class Assignment No. 2 of 2025; Thailand NBTC 920 to 925 MHz notification; Indonesia Permenkominfo 2/2023; Vietnam Circular 08/2021/TT-BTTTT |

- **US.** 15.247(a)(2) asks a digitally modulated system for a 6 dB bandwidth of at least 500 kHz, so the trunk runs LoRa at 500 kHz. A fixed 125 kHz channel with 2.3 s frames meets neither that rule nor the hopping one (at least 50 channels, at most 0.4 s on each), and 15.249 allows about a milliwatt. The power spectral density limit, 8 dBm in any 3 kHz (15.247(e)), binds before the 1 W of (b)(3): spread flat over 500 kHz, 30 dBm would be 7.8 dBm in 3 kHz, with 0.2 dB to spare and none for the ripple of a chirp's spectrum, so the profile stops at 28 dBm conducted, 2 dB under, and an antenna of more than 6 dBi takes its excess off that, as (b)(4) does for the power. The rule sets no duty limit; the trunk keeps itself to a quarter of the air so a shared channel stays usable. 919.0 MHz sits between LoRaWAN US915's highest uplink channel (914.9 MHz) and its lowest downlink (923.3 MHz), so the 500 kHz channel overlaps neither.
- **EU.** The 869.40 to 869.65 MHz sub-band allows 500 mW e.r.p. at up to 10 % duty over one hour, every emission counted. 869.525 MHz centers a 125 kHz channel in it with 62.5 kHz to spare each side. A 25 % duty is legal only at 5 mW, in 869.70 to 870.00 MHz, which the trunk does not use.
- **India.** GSR 853(E) of 2021 replaced the 2005 and 2008 notifications: 865 to 868 MHz, 500 mW e.r.p., adaptive power control required, at most 2.5 % duty for a device that is not a network access point, 200 kHz at most. 866.6 MHz lies between the RFID channels at 866.3 and 866.9 MHz and inside both the 2008 band and the 2021 one.
- **Australia and New Zealand.** The LIPD Class Licence 2025 replaced the 2015 one from 1 October 2025 and kept its 915 to 928 MHz rule for digital modulation: 1 W e.i.r.p., and a radiated peak power spectral density of 25 mW in any 3 kHz. It sets no minimum bandwidth, duty limit or dwell time. At 30 dBm over 500 kHz the density is 7.8 dBm in 3 kHz, 6 dB under the limit, read as an average across the band as the US profile's 15.247(e) figure is; LoRaWAN's AU915 plan sends its SF12, 500 kHz downlinks at up to the same 30 dBm under this class license. New Zealand's short range device license allows 1 W e.i.r.p. in 915 to 928 MHz for any modulation, so the profile meets both. 919.0 MHz sits between the AU915 sub-band and AS923 channels in use there and below the AU915 downlinks, and the trunk keeps the US profile's quarter of the air.
- **Southeast Asia.** Singapore (917 to 925 MHz), Malaysia (919 to 923 MHz), Thailand (920 to 925 MHz), Indonesia (920 to 923 MHz) and Vietnam (920 to 923 MHz) all open 920 to 923 MHz, under different limits. The profile takes the strictest of each: Vietnam's 25 mW e.r.p. without a frequency license, the 1 % duty Singapore, Indonesia and Vietnam set for an end device (Thailand's at or below 50 mW), and Indonesia's 250 kHz ceiling on bandwidth. 922.0 MHz is inside every one of those bands and below Malaysia's 923 to 924 MHz, which asks for listen before talk or 1 % duty. A 1 % budget is 36 s an hour: about 28 full frames, or many more short messages, which is why the profile runs SF9.

The conducted power the node configures is the region's ceiling less the antenna gain set in Kconfig (`AH_LORA_ANTENNA_GAIN_DDBI`, 3 dBi by default), never above what the board's RF path delivers: 22 dBm from a bare SX1262, 28 dBm through the Heltec V4's front end (whose SX1262 is never driven above 21 dBm). The SX1262's lowest setting, -9 dBm, comes out of the V4's front end at 2 to 4 dBm, so a V4 in `sea` with an antenna of about 12 dBi or more has no legal power, and the node does not start its trunk. **An antenna of more gain than the setting breaks the ceiling: set it to the antenna fitted.**

### What Cannot Comply, or Has Not Been Checked

- The US profile has the fixed-channel bandwidth argument above, but no Airlink build has been through FCC equipment authorization; the boards' own modular grants cover their stated configurations, which a 500 kHz DTS mode at the module's power is assumed, not verified, to be within.
- The US and Australian power spectral density figures read the density as an average over time, as KDB 558074's average methods measure a constant-envelope signal. Its peak method, which it also accepts, reads a chirp as it sweeps through each 3 kHz, and the slow sweep of SF12 much higher: a 500 kHz LoRa DTS test report shows a peak density only about 12 dB under the total power. A profile measured that way would need far less power; the bench measures both ([TESTING.md](TESTING.md), S17).
- The EU profile's 10 % is the sub-band's limit for one device; a fleet in one place shares one channel.
- The Philippines has no profile: 915 to 918 MHz is license-free only indoors, at 250 mW e.r.p. with no external antenna, and 920 to 925 MHz is assigned to broadcast links. Other countries the `sea` profile does not name (Brunei, Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, Timor-Leste) have not been checked, nor has Korea, Japan or anywhere else without a profile. Adding one means citing its rule here first.
- Each Southeast Asian country also certifies or registers radio equipment (Singapore's IMDA registration, Malaysia's MCMC certification, Thailand's type approval, and Indonesia's and Vietnam's schemes); the profile meets the spectrum rule, not the paperwork for a product.
- Australia's class license leaves "digital modulation" undefined; the profile takes LoRa's chirp spread spectrum to qualify, as LoRaWAN deployments there do. Thailand's duty figures come from its technical standard as published by compliance services, not from the gazette text.
- The trunk has no frequency hopping and no listen-before-talk regime of its own; a region that requires either cannot be supported by adding a channel alone.
